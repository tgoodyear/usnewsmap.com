# The OCR quality audit's own word lists

`quality.py` (metric v2) scores a page against its language's most frequent words. wordfreq supplies
them for most languages; these files cover languages it has no list for (`LOCAL_WORDLISTS`). Each is
the 5,000 most frequent words, most frequent first, one per line, folded as `quality.py` folds a page's
words. Rebuild them with `ocr/ja-ocr/tools/build-wordlists.py <code>`.

| `yi.txt`  | Yiddish  | Article pages of [Yiddish Wikipedia](https://yi.wikipedia.org/) and [Yiddish Wikisource](https://yi.wikisource.org/) (the `latest` dumps: about 3.5 million and 2.4 million words)                                                                                                                                                                                        | CC BY-SA 4.0 (Wikimedia contributors); this list is derived from them and shared under the same license | 6 October 2026  |
| `chr.txt` | Cherokee | The Cherokee New Testament (the 1860 American Bible Society translation, Worcester and Boudinot), typeset by Michael Joyner and Timothy Legg, revised 24 February 2015: the large and small print PDFs (`cntl.pdf`, sha256 `937cdb9b…4f83`; [`cnts.pdf`](https://todaysnative.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/cnts.pdf), sha256 `54fd0685…88ca`; about 115,700 words each) | CC0                                                                                                     | 5 November 2026 |

Folding:

- **Hawaiian:** the ʻokina and the kahakō are dropped, because 19th-century Hawaiian papers didn't print them ("olelo" for "ʻōlelo"). Only words of (consonant-)vowel syllables are kept, which drops the corpus's English.
- **Yiddish:** vowel points and other marks are dropped, and the ligatures װ, ױ and ײ are written as their two letters, as `quality.py` does with a page's words.
- **Cherokee:** casefolding takes the syllabary's small letters (U+AB70–ABBF, U+13F8–13FD) to its capitals, which is how the PDFs and 19th-century papers print it. The text is the PDF's text layer (`pdftotext`), from the first chapter on, without the running heads and chapter headings. A word broken across lines or by a verse number in the margin leaves two pieces, and the editions break their lines in different places, so each word counts as often as it does in the edition it is fewest in (`build-wordlists.py chr --pdf cntl.pdf --pdf cnts.pdf`; with no `--pdf` it downloads `cnts.pdf` alone, which gives the same top 100).

Limits:

- The Yiddish list is mostly modern Wikipedia text, in modern spelling. American Yiddish papers before the 1930s often spelled German-style ("דיא" for "די"), so some of their real words may score as damage. Wikisource's older texts cover part of that.
- The Hawaiian corpus is mostly 19th- and 20th-century text, with some OCR errors of its own.
- The Cherokee list is one book's vocabulary: it covers 31.8% of the words in the Library of Congress's OCR of the Cherokee Phoenix (1828–34, 324 pages), and the top 5,000 are 80.5% of the book's words. Names the Bible lacks are missing (ᏣᎳᎩ, "Cherokee", the paper's own name). The Phoenix's OCR confuses letters of the period's type: it reads Ꮅ as Ꮲ, Ꮧ as Ꮨ, Ꮩ as Ꭺ and ᎾᏍᎩ ("that") as ᏩᏍᎩ, ᏣᏍᎩ or ᏳᏍᎩ, and those count as damage. `build-wordlists.py chr-check --ocr <dir or batch tar>` reports, for a set of OCR text, the share of its words in the list, its letter substitutions and its near-misses of the top 20.
- Two other sources were evaluated and not used (#1581): the Cherokee Language Consortium word list (no licence; modern coinages, with Western and Eastern dialect forms marked (w) and (e)) and the textbook _We Are Learning Cherokee_ (all rights reserved; written phonetically in the Latin alphabet).

Not covered: Choctaw, Dakota, Lakota and Navajo. No open frequency list of useful size exists for them; their pages stay undetermined or are read as English.

German needs no list of its own. `ocr/ja-ocr/tools/build-wordlists.py ger-check` checks whether 19th-century spellings would count as damage, using the [Deutsches Textarchiv](https://www.deutschestextarchiv.de/)'s 1800–1899 texts. They don't. "daß", "Theil", "giebt" and "seyn" are not one edit from German's 20 most common words. The near-misses those texts use most are "fich", "fie" and "ift" (the long s read as f), and those are OCR damage.
